Lost in Translation

Memoir

I went mute. The world didn't stop, but my place in it did. My brain stalled like a flooded engine while a circle of aunties – unfamiliar, smelling of jasmine oil and tiger balm – stared at me. They were waiting. They were expecting a daughter who mirrored her mother's silver-tongued grace. Instead, they found a void. Their eyebrows, plucked into skeptical arches, said everything: Why is she silent?

I looked past them, trying to anchor myself in the room. The house was a temple of noise: piles of jackets and shoes, some neatly arranged, others spilling toward the door; kids and elders reuniting; oranges stacked as offerings; prayer mats occupied by bowed heads; the scent of pho floating in the air. Voices bounced off the ceramic floor tiles, a music I recognized but could not compose. I stood in the center of the roar and felt as if I were underwater.

My head snapped back. Be polite. Be presentable. Be better. I scavenged the dusty corners of my memory for Vietnamese syllables. I needed a bridge. Finally, a single question emerged from the chatter tossed at me like a coin to a beggar.

"Con bao nhiêu tuổi?" (How old are you?)

Numbers. They are the only part of a language that stays in a straight line. I counted the ghosts of my fingers in my head until I reached ten.

"Mười," I stammered.

The word felt heavy and misshapen in my mouth, tumbling out with the wrong tone. One auntie let out a thin, sharp chuckle. I'm not sure if she found it cute or amusing. She said something to the woman beside her, something about my "American" tongue, my flat vowels. The Vietnamese weekend schools had failed; I was a tourist in my own heritage. They didn't stop. They pressed on, asking about school, about grades, about a life I couldn't translate.

I couldn't retreat because their eyes were a physical weight, pinning me to the spot. I looked down at my feet. Sweat prickled against the high collar of my áo dài. My fingers twitched, my nails digging into the soft, unforgiving silk.

"Tốt" (Good.)

It was a pathetic, one-syllable shield.

Across the room, I caught a glimpse of my mother. She was the sun around which the party orbited, gesturing effortlessly, her laugh a bright, melodic ribbon that tied the room together. People leaned into her, charmed by the very cadence that I was currently butchering. She saw me. She saw my drowning and she didn't move. She didn't rescue me. She simply watched, her expression an unreadable mask of expectation. She wanted me to find the shore on my own.

I faked a polite nod, a hollow imitation of her face, and slipped away. I didn't walk so much as shrink, retreating into the shadow she cast across the tile. Their voices rise and fall in rhythmic Vietnamese, seamless and familiar, the kind that makes me feel like I am eavesdropping. It was a beautiful song, and I was the only one in the room who didn't know the lyrics.

Rearview Mirror

Flash Fiction

The morning was cold and gray, with the crisp chill of a bright winter day; people were shivering on the sidewalk and the trees were bare, changing the original fall scene into a cold winter day. My fingers stayed stiff on the wheel and the windows fogged at the edges. I watched as my breath turned into small white clouds, drifting off before they fully formed. Outside, people moved with their heads down, coats pulled tight, bags held close against their sides.

I've driven this route long enough to know who gets on where and what kind of day they've had before they tap their card. Or at least, I thought I did.

Sometimes when the brakes screech at the light, I can still see the tide rising over eastern highways and the headlights marching into the city, an endless red thread. I see the factory workers from up north with their hands shoved into deep empty pockets, eyes wide open, staring straight through the glass as if they're still watching the assembly lines lock their doors for good. And the boy near the front doors. I watch him, stare down at an old man who looks like he might collapse if the bus takes a sharp turn. A look of fierce, silent concentration sits on his face as he watches him sway. Why don't you offer him a hand? I want to ask him, but I already know why. He knows what happens: you stand up until your spine breaks, you miss your stop, you don't eat. He hated the weakness in kindness that much.

At my next transfer stop, the crowd thickened into something reckless. I sighed and waited. The regulars climbed on.

"Morning, Ruth."

Ruth had been riding this bus for years. She used to board with a thermos and a newspaper back when people still made small talk. Now, she leaned hard on her cane, a plastic bag pulling her wrist down. Behind her came an unfamiliar face. One hand braced under her belly, the coat stretched tight across it. A new face. Strange. I know my regulars. The limping man in the faded Red Sox cap, Herbert, who lost his pension. The woman who smells like cleaning supplies and hums to herself to block out the noise. People cling to routine in crisis instead of changing their routes. I looked back at the road. The light changed. I pulled away from the curb. I don't look at the passengers. I'm not supposed to. We're trained to keep moving unless someone's screaming or bleeding. But I listened. I always listen.

"I saw that seat first," Ruth said.

"You've got to be joking," the pregnant woman said.

"You're in the way."

A mutter from somewhere in the middle. "She's making it worse."

Sometimes riders argued or fought on board over the stupidest things. I stayed out of it because interfering makes my workday drag.

"She's old."

"She's pregnant."

"We're all tired."

Ruth gripped the metal pole. "If I fall and don't get back up. That'll be on whoever pushed me out of the way."

The bus took a sharp turn. Both women held on, knuckles white. A teenager near the front smirked, nudging her friend.

The pregnant woman kept her voice low, "Please let me sit. I've been standing all day."

"You'll manage." Ruth said.

"You don't even know me."

People shifted. Herbert moved his eyes from one woman to the next.

"Ruth," he said gently, "maybe let her—"

"Herbert. Don't."

"But the baby—"

"Hey, that was my stop!" someone yelled from the back, trapped behind the wall of bodies. "Just sit down or move!"

Both women talked like they were the reasonable ones, the ones being forced into this. Neither wanted to be the person who took something from someone else. Neither wanted to look like the problem.

The pregnant woman swallowed, hand circling her stomach. "I don't want a fight. I just want to sit."

A man brushed past her, shoving hard. "No space," he muttered, though there certainly was room.

Ruth spoke again, the fight draining out of her. "Listen, lady, my knees are done. Let me sit."

The younger woman answered, staring at the scarred rubber floor. "Yeah. Well. Mine too."

I kept my eyes on the road. I have seen people ignore each other for years, pushing without apology is normal now. Still, I wished I could stop the bus and make them stop. Then, at the next stop, a young guy in a faded work jacket stood up from his seat without a word, without making eye contact with anyone. He just grabbed his bag, stepped into the crowded aisle, and gave up his place of safety. The pregnant woman sat. Before she could get comfortable, she shifted over, pressing her shoulder hard against the cold window, leaving exactly half the plastic seat open.

She looked up. "Sit," she said.

Ruth hesitated, then lowered herself, squeezing into the tight space. Neither of them looked comfortable, but they were off their feet. The bus was quiet again, as it should be. Nobody cheered because nobody actually cared. One person giving up a seat wasn't going to fix a collapsing city, and tomorrow they'd all be back to fighting for the same six inches of floor space. Nevertheless, Herbert nudged his bag aside just enough so the young guy had enough space to lean against the handrail. At the transfer stop, Ruth stood to exit. She reached into her heavy plastic bag and pulled out a small, perfectly round, unbruised orange, gently placing it onto the pregnant woman's lap.

"For the baby," she said, her eyes focused on the floor.

The pregnant woman didn't argue. Her hand settled over the bright rind, a faint, tired smile finally cracking her expression. "Thank you."

I watched as Ruth stepped down to the curb, pulling her scarf up against the wind, giving me a nod. As I listened to the engine and the quietness, I figured I could at least make it through another day.

The Swallow

Flash Fiction

The bird arrived after the storm, fluttering in through the open window, its tiny beak pressed against the glass. It was a small thing, the color of a bruise just beginning to turn blue. Inside the room, Tori was a mess of salt and tremors. When she unlatched the window, the wind rushed in with the scent of ozone. She scooped the innocent creature into an old shoebox, the cardboard damp under her palms.

"Safe," she rasped. "I'll keep you quiet."

It didn't struggle. It settled, its tiny heart thrumming against the box like a muffled drum. Tori watched it, certain this was her penance turned into a prize.

"There is a smell of wet feathers in here, child." Her grandmother stood in the doorway, her gaze drifting toward the crinkled sheets. "And your eyes are red. Is everything alright?"

Tori's pulse spiked. The memory of the shed, the fire she had invited watching the wood curl into a blackened bone while her brother took the blame, began to climb her throat.

She reached into the box lifting the bird. It was warm, impossibly soft. Tori unhinged her jaw until the hinges ached. She didn't chew. She simply let the blue feathered weight slide down, a slow motion drowning in reverse. When it reached the space behind her sternum, it expanded, smoothing the fire in her head.

But the bird was a glutton. It didn't want her silence; it wanted to be fed.

A month later, her father sat at the kitchen table, his forehead resting in the wells of his palms. He was mourning a loss Tori had authored, though he called it an act of God.

"What did we do wrong for God to punish us?" he whispered.

Inside her, the bird thrashed. A sharp, calcium-thin beak poked at the soft tissue of her esophagus. She felt a drop of internal warmth, blood, perhaps, trickle down toward her stomach. To still the bird, she had to feed it. She had to weave a web of "I was asleep" and "The door was locked."

She became a master of curated distance. She stopped hugging her mother because the bird's wings beat too hard against her ribs, and the sound was a frantic, wet thudding she was sure others could hear. People remarked on her "composure," unaware that her grace was held together by the tension of a caged animal.

At night, the bird hummed a low vibration in her ribs that made her feel chosen. Loved.

The end arrived with the clink of Sunday silverware. Every chime of a fork against a plate was a needle. The truth was a mountain of feathers pushing against the back of her tongue.

"You look so pale, darling. Are you ill?" her mother asked.

Tori opened her mouth to lie, but the bird had grown too dense for the narrow passage.

She felt a rib crack, a dull, internal snap. It had grown on a diet of shadows. It wanted out.

She fled to the bathroom, clutching the faucet until her knuckles turned white. Her throat was bulging, the skin stretched thin enough to see the ghost of a wing-tip pulse beneath the surface.

She opened her mouth, and the "peace" left her in a violent, jagged rush.

It was no longer blue. It was a wet, glistening knot of bone and grey down, coughing itself up into the porcelain basin. It was slick with the red evidence of her internal tearing. The thing laid in the sink, eyes milky and twisted, its chest heaving with the evidence of everything she'd swallowed.

Tori stood over the drain, her mouth copper-tasting and raw. The house was silent now, a cold silence. The warmth beneath her ribs had vanished, leaving a hollow cavern where her heart used to be.

Vignette Series

Sixteen small windows — five of my favorites

A series of short vignettes about family, language, and the objects that carry culture across an ocean.

My Mother's Name

Vignette · 01

In Vietnamese my mother's first name is Huyền. It means mysterious or sometimes black, like the color her hair used to be. Then my father met her and called her Mumi, and the name stuck like rice on a pan. She says it means angel. She decided that herself. It is only the nickname he gave her twenty-seven years ago, but she has used it ever since.

Huyền became a name nobody could pronounce. The reason why teachers never called on her. The reason she decided to change it. Nowadays, she acts as if Huyền was never hers. Mumi is who she is now. Only my grandmother still says the old name, soft and full in her mouth. I think it's because she misses it.

She named me after a girl in a movie who sparkles in the sun and falls in love with a vampire. I never liked that girl. But in Italian the name means beautiful, so I keep it.

My parents never even considered giving me a Vietnamese name. People still ask anyway. I tell them no. I am only Bella. American on paper, Vietnamese in the kitchen, and somehow neither when I open my mouth.

Jade Bracelet

Vignette · 02

I could never take it off. Jade, the color of wet leaves after rain, has left its own pale ring around my wrist.

My grandmother's bracelet sits loosely now, shifting whenever she moves her hand. My mother's rests against her skin, a thin white vein splitting the green like an old scar. Mine has not slipped off my hand in years.

They say that's what jade does. It absorbs the bad luck, the sharp words, the long nights. When it cracks, it has done its work. I turn it around and around when I am nervous. I sleep with it. I shower with it. I play tennis with it.

Sometimes I press my thumb against the cool surface and think of my grandmother, my aunts, my mother, each of them touching the same smooth circle years before me. All of us, linked by one unbroken circle of green.

The Photograph

Vignette · 03

In the picture her face is serious. Her mouth is a straight line. She is not smiling for the camera; she is looking past it, calculating the next thing that needs to be done.

I met her only once, when I was a baby. I have no memory of the weight of her arms, the sound of her voice, or the scent of her clothes. I know her through a single photograph and the stories my mother and grandmother keep telling.

They say she raised five children alone after her husband died. They say she ran a chain selling motorbikes at the same time, refusing to let grief decide the rest of her life. When I look at her face, I wonder if that is what I am seeing.

When she died, my mother booked a flight to Vietnam that same night. It had been fifteen years since she had set foot on Vietnamese soil. I stayed behind. I never made it to the funeral.

Sometimes I think about how strange it is to miss someone I never really knew. I look at her photograph and search for pieces of myself: a familiar pair of eyes, the shape of a jaw, a kind of determination I hope I inherited.

Cà Phê Trứng

Vignette · 04

I hate coffee. I hate the bitter, burnt taste that leaves a dark shadow on your tongue, the kind of drink people force themselves to love just to feel like adults. So when my dad handed me a small glass topped with a thick, heavy foam, I hesitated.

"Try it," he said, smiling.

The first sip was not coffee. It was a liquid, velvet, sweet, and rich whipped custard, melting into the dark, intense espresso hiding beneath it. It was creamy, warm, and entirely unexpected. Now whenever anyone tries to tell me coffee is just bitter water, I think of the woman standing in the kitchen, whisking egg yolks and condensed milk until they turned into gold, proving that no one does coffee like we do.

Now, I still don't drink coffee. But whenever I see egg foam settling over dark espresso, I think of Vietnam. Funny how home can change the taste of something.

Áo Dài

Vignette · 05

The áo dài was a trap. It's the high, stiff collar that pinches your neck, the endless line of tiny buttons you can never snap by yourself, and the long, flowing panels that caught on the chair legs every time you tried to sit down. My mother would smooth the silk over my shoulders for New Year, and all I could think about was how fast I could take it off to put on my hoodie and sweatpants.

But today, as I pull the soft fabric over my head, I stand a little taller in the mirror. The long silk is light, moving like water over the fitted pants underneath. What used to feel restrictive now feels like armor. I watch as the fabric catches the light, marveling at how something so delicate can make me feel so entirely unbroken.

Beyond the Screen

Feature · Tutors for a Cause

By Bella Duong · Photography by Rocco Ho

For four years, Thao My occupied the same exact square on my laptop screen. She was seven years old when we started — quiet, guarded, and quick to retreat into the background when she didn't feel confident enough to speak. When I called on her, her eyes dropped immediately. However, sometimes the internet froze before she found the words. I taught her vocabulary, guided her through sentence structures, and then, with a simple click, I logged off. When the screen went dark, so did my view of her life.

The classroom at Phu Hai School in Hue, Vietnam, runs its English lessons over a shared video connection. Students sit in rows looking at individual monitors. When the signal drops, and it drops often, two teachers stand at the front, waiting for it to return. The students wait in silence with them, and almost no one speaks.

I have been on the other end of that connection since eighth grade, volunteering as a remote tutor through Tutors for a Cause. My parents arrived in the United States from Vietnam more than twenty years ago as international students. They often tell my sister and I that learning English changed the course of their lives, opening doors they never imagined possible. Growing up, they reminded us that whatever education gives us, we should find ways to pass on to someone else. Tutoring became one way I tried to do that.

For four years, I knew exactly who answered the questions and who didn't. But I never saw the homes they returned to, what they were working toward, or what stood in their way.

This past summer, now a rising senior, I decided to change that. I interviewed four students: Nhat Huy, Uyem Nhi, Phuc Hieu, and Thao My, all between ten and twelve years old. What they told me forced me to reconsider what I had assumed.

None of the students own a computer. To speak with me, they traveled to Phu Hai Middle School and waited in the computer lab. When we finally connected, the desktop had no sound. Their faces appeared on my screen, but I was unable to hear anything. A teacher disappeared from the room and minutes passed. Then, he returned with a laptop borrowed from somewhere else in the school, and we started again.

When I was finally able to greet them in English, Thao My smiled politely, but she kept looking down, puzzled. I assumed the connection had frozen, but it hadn't. I was speaking too quickly, and she and the other students were gathering the courage to tell me so. After that, when speaking became difficult, they switched to writing by carefully composing answers. We spent the rest of the interview that way. It was the most thoughtful and meaningful conversation I shared with them in four years.

Their stories made the limitations of their classroom easier to understand. Learning English depended on the technology available that day, the books they could access, and the time they could spend practicing outside school. None of these obstacles seemed enormous on its own. Together, they shaped what learning could look like.

Thao My loves a green field near her house. She said it is lush enough to look like a carpet of grass. She goes there after school, runs around, breathes the air, and plays games with the kids from her neighborhood. She described it in a way you know that is where she belonged.

Her dream is to become an interpreter so she can "move between languages naturally, without hesitation." Hearing that stuck with me because I had spent four years watching her hesitate.

The version of these students I knew from the screen was real, but thin. Uyen Nhi memorizes every word she learns by writing it out by hand in a notebook. Without apps or intent practice at home, she keeps writing until the word stays. Phuc Hieu practices without an English book because he doesn't have one. Thao My wants a tablet. These are not dramatic deprivations. They are small things that accumulate into something larger over years.

What I hadn't understood, until they told me, was the anxiety behind those silences. Thao My remembered being "very anxious, very nervous, and a bit shy" in our early sessions. Phuc Hieu described the unease of his first conversation with a tutor from outside Vietnam.

Hearing this, I thought of all the times I had waited on my end of the screen — the long silences and the way a student would go still when they forgot the word they needed. I had read those silences as hesitation. They were something closer to courage.

Their futures, when they named them, were not tentative. Thao My wants to become an interpreter and earn enough to take her whole family to other countries. "My big dream," she said. Uyen Nhi wants to study in the United States and build a career as a pianist. Phuc Hieu wants to become a doctor. Nhật Huy wants to fly to the moon, while also wanting to become a doctor and helping his grandparents wash dishes in the evenings. He said it plainly as if the scale of it required no special emphasis.

At the end of our conversations, I asked Uyen Nhi what she would say to a student who wanted to learn but had no books, no tablet, and no reliable internet. "Keep trying," she told me, "because there is always someone ready to encourage you in any circumstance."

Our online connection was real, but it only showed a bit of the truth. This summer, I caught a glimpse of the lives continuing long after the laptop is shut: the lush green field, the handwritten notebooks, and the quiet rows of shoes left outside the classroom door.

My parents crossed an ocean believing that education could change a life. Meeting Thao My and the Phu Hai students reminded me that, for many children, that same possibility begins with something much smaller: a borrowed laptop, a handwritten notebook, and the courage to speak one unfamiliar sentence at a time. For four years, I thought I knew the girl inside that small square on my screen. I had really only known the silence before she found her voice.
