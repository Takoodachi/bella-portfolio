Beyond the Screen

Feature · Tutors for a Cause

By Bella Duong · Photography by Rocco Ho

For four years, Thao My occupied the same exact square on my laptop screen. She was seven years old when we started — quiet, guarded, and quick to retreat into the background when she didn't feel confident enough to speak. When I called on her, her eyes dropped immediately. However, sometimes the internet froze before she found the words. I taught her vocabulary, guided her through sentence structures, and then, with a simple click, I logged off. When the screen went dark, so did my view of her life.

The classroom at Phu Hai School in Hue, Vietnam, runs its English lessons over a shared video connection. Students sit in rows looking at individual monitors. When the signal drops, and it drops often, two teachers stand at the front, waiting for it to return. The students wait in silence with them, and almost no one speaks.

I have been on the other end of that connection since eighth grade, volunteering as a remote tutor through Tutors for a Cause. My parents arrived in the United States from Vietnam more than twenty years ago as international students. They often tell my sister and I that learning English changed the course of their lives, opening doors they never imagined possible. Growing up, they reminded us that whatever education gives us, we should find ways to pass on to someone else. Tutoring became one way I tried to do that.

For four years, I knew exactly who answered the questions and who didn't. But I never saw the homes they returned to, what they were working toward, or what stood in their way.

This past summer, now a rising senior, I decided to change that. I interviewed four students: Nhat Huy, Uyem Nhi, Phuc Hieu, and Thao My, all between ten and twelve years old. What they told me forced me to reconsider what I had assumed.

None of the students own a computer. To speak with me, they traveled to Phu Hai Middle School and waited in the computer lab. When we finally connected, the desktop had no sound. Their faces appeared on my screen, but I was unable to hear anything. A teacher disappeared from the room and minutes passed. Then, he returned with a laptop borrowed from somewhere else in the school, and we started again.

When I was finally able to greet them in English, Thao My smiled politely, but she kept looking down, puzzled. I assumed the connection had frozen, but it hadn't. I was speaking too quickly, and she and the other students were gathering the courage to tell me so. After that, when speaking became difficult, they switched to writing by carefully composing answers. We spent the rest of the interview that way. It was the most thoughtful and meaningful conversation I shared with them in four years.

Their stories made the limitations of their classroom easier to understand. Learning English depended on the technology available that day, the books they could access, and the time they could spend practicing outside school. None of these obstacles seemed enormous on its own. Together, they shaped what learning could look like.

Thao My loves a green field near her house. She said it is lush enough to look like a carpet of grass. She goes there after school, runs around, breathes the air, and plays games with the kids from her neighborhood. She described it in a way you know that is where she belonged.

Her dream is to become an interpreter so she can "move between languages naturally, without hesitation." Hearing that stuck with me because I had spent four years watching her hesitate.

The version of these students I knew from the screen was real, but thin. Uyen Nhi memorizes every word she learns by writing it out by hand in a notebook. Without apps or intent practice at home, she keeps writing until the word stays. Phuc Hieu practices without an English book because he doesn't have one. Thao My wants a tablet. These are not dramatic deprivations. They are small things that accumulate into something larger over years.

What I hadn't understood, until they told me, was the anxiety behind those silences. Thao My remembered being "very anxious, very nervous, and a bit shy" in our early sessions. Phuc Hieu described the unease of his first conversation with a tutor from outside Vietnam.

Hearing this, I thought of all the times I had waited on my end of the screen — the long silences and the way a student would go still when they forgot the word they needed. I had read those silences as hesitation. They were something closer to courage.

Their futures, when they named them, were not tentative. Thao My wants to become an interpreter and earn enough to take her whole family to other countries. "My big dream," she said. Uyen Nhi wants to study in the United States and build a career as a pianist. Phuc Hieu wants to become a doctor. Nhật Huy wants to fly to the moon, while also wanting to become a doctor and helping his grandparents wash dishes in the evenings. He said it plainly as if the scale of it required no special emphasis.

At the end of our conversations, I asked Uyen Nhi what she would say to a student who wanted to learn but had no books, no tablet, and no reliable internet. "Keep trying," she told me, "because there is always someone ready to encourage you in any circumstance."

Our online connection was real, but it only showed a bit of the truth. This summer, I caught a glimpse of the lives continuing long after the laptop is shut: the lush green field, the handwritten notebooks, and the quiet rows of shoes left outside the classroom door.

My parents crossed an ocean believing that education could change a life. Meeting Thao My and the Phu Hai students reminded me that, for many children, that same possibility begins with something much smaller: a borrowed laptop, a handwritten notebook, and the courage to speak one unfamiliar sentence at a time. For four years, I thought I knew the girl inside that small square on my screen. I had really only known the silence before she found her voice.

A New Era of National Security

I co-founded The Governor's Review, our school's first political and economic journal, and edit it today. This is my feature from the Spring 2026 issue — turn the pages to read it.

Political Economy

A New Era of National Security

The expansion of national security into economic life has kindled a transformation of U.S. governance.

By Bella Duong

For decades, American national security was defined by relatively narrow terms: defending physical borders from military threats and terrorism. Today, that definition has expanded to include domains that would have once been considered purely economic. From artificial intelligence to pharmaceutical supply chains and even the price of consumer goods, the boundary between military defense and domestic economic policy has increasingly faded. Driven by shifting strategic doctrine and reinforced by recent legal developments, the executive branch now routinely uses “national security” to justify intervention in trade, energy, and technology. This expansion of what can be understood as “security inflation” is a transformation in how the U.S. government defines risk, power, and its own role in the economy.

Origins of Expansion

The expansion of national security is the result of a series of overlapping shocks that exposed the fragility of the global economic system. Rising geopolitical competition between the United States and China has reframed economic interdependence as a strategic risk. Technologies such as artificial intelligence became a place of competition. At the same time, the COVID-19 pandemic revealed the extent to which critical supply chains were reduced abroad. Shortages of medical equipment, pharmaceuticals, and basic goods demonstrated that efficiency-driven globalization had created hidden issues. More recently, geopolitical instability has reinforced this perspective. Disruptions to global energy markets and trade routes, including tensions surrounding the Strait of Hormuz, have shown how quickly distant conflicts can turn into domestic economic consequences. Together, these developments have encouraged policymakers to treat economic systems as strategic infrastructures requiring protection and control.

Redefining “National Security”

At the core of this shift is a fundamental redefinition of what counts as a threat. The United States Trade Representative’s (USTR) 2026 Trade Policy Agenda explicitly states that “economic security is national security,” signaling that economic systems are now viewed as integral to the defense strategy. Federal agencies have begun to operationalize this broader definition in concrete ways. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) now identifies cyber-attacks on critical infrastructure, artificial intelligence misuse, intellectual property theft, climate disruptions, and pandemics as active national security threats. What links these diverse risks is their potential to disrupt the functioning of the state. In this sense, vulnerability is no longer defined by physical exposure, but by systemic dependence.

This shift is reinforced by broader changes in U.S. strategic thinking. Analyses of recent National Security Strategy (NSS) frameworks by the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) show a move away from prioritizing global democratic expansion toward a more pragmatic focus on economic and technological dominance. Maintaining leadership in industries such as artificial intelligence, quantum computing, and energy is now treated as a strategic defense asset requiring government oversight.

This represents a historic departure from the late-20th-century consensus of free-market globalization. Rather than assuming that open markets produce stability, policymakers now view reliance on foreign supply chains as a structural vulnerability. The role of the state is shifting accordingly, from facilitating markets to actively shaping them. The question is no longer whether the government should intervene, but where and how.

The Healthcare Paradox

The consequences of this shift become most visible when applied to specific industries. The pharmaceutical supply chain offers a particularly revealing case. Currently, the United States relies heavily on geopolitical rivals, such as China and India for Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients (APIs), the essential components required for most medications. From a traditional economic perspective, this arrangement reflects efficiency: production occurs where it is cheapest. From a security perspective, however, it represents a dangerous dependency. In response, policymakers have proposed tariffs and domestic production mandates to reduce reliance on foreign suppliers. Yet these measures expose a central tension within security-driven policy. While intended to strengthen resilience, they can generate immediate and tangible costs. Tariffs designed to incentivize domestic production increase manufacturing expenses, raising the risk of drug shortages in the short term.

This condition becomes even more noticeable in the application of Section 232 tariffs, which allows the executive branch to restrict imports on national security grounds. When applied to raw materials such as steel, aluminum, and specialized plastics, these tariffs raise the manufacturing cost of producing essential medical equipment, such as hospital beds and syringes. In this way, a policy justified in the name of security can undermine another part of it. If public health is itself a national security concern, then increasing the cost of healthcare infrastructure complicates the very logic used to justify intervention.

Executive Power and Economic Consequences

This expansion of national security into economic policy has also altered the balance of power within the federal government. A recent 2026 Supreme Court decision has granted the executive branch broad discretion in defining what constitutes a national security threat in the context of trade. By limiting judicial oversight, the ruling effectively allows the president to impose tariffs and reshape industrial policy with minimal constraint, so long as these actions are framed in terms of security.

This legal shift amplifies the effects of security inflation. As the definition of security expands, so too does the scope of executive authority. Policies that would traditionally require legislative debate can now be implemented unilaterally, justified by the urgency of national defense.

At the same time, these policies carry broader economic consequences. Efforts to reorganize supply chains around resilience rather than efficiency reflect a global transformation. The Observer Research Foundation describes the transition from “just-in-time” to “just-in-case” production models. While this shift may reduce exposure to external shocks, it also introduces higher costs into the system, contributing to long-term inflationary pressures. In prioritizing security, policymakers are effectively accepting economic trade-offs as a permanent feature of the system rather than a temporary response to the crisis.

Conclusion

The merging of economic policy and national security marks a fundamental transformation in American governance. As the definition of security continues to expand, it becomes an increasingly powerful justification for state intervention across nearly every sector of the economy. However, while the expansion of national security reflects real issues, it also risks normalizing a permanent place of economic intervention justified by crisis. If nearly any policy can be framed as a matter of security, the distinction between necessity and political choice begins to go away. Ultimately, security inflation redefines the boundaries of political authority. In expanding what counts as security, the United States is constructing a new model of governance in which the logic of defense increasingly structures everyday economic life. ■
